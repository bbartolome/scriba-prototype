# Chapter 2: What Counts as Practice

When people say they practise, they often mean that they do the activity again. They play the same piece through, run the same route, or write another page in the same style. That repetition has its place. The question this chapter asks is whether it is the kind of practice that changes what a person can do.

A 1993 article by K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, published in _Psychological Review_, draws the line this chapter needs. On one side is playing through what you can already do. On the other side is deliberate practice, which the article describes by a few clear features.

Deliberate practice, as the article describes it, works on a well-defined task set at the right level of difficulty. It gives informative feedback, so the learner can see what went wrong. It leaves room to repeat the task and to correct the errors that the feedback shows.

The contrast with playing through is the useful part. Playing through means repeating a skill you have already reached, at the level you already work at. Deliberate practice works on a task at the right level of difficulty, with the feedback and the repetition described above.

For a reader, the test is simple to state. Look at a session and ask a few plain questions: was the task chosen for the level you are at, did you learn what went wrong, and did you get a chance to try again? A session that answers yes to these questions has the features the article describes.

The distinction is the useful part for the rest of this book. It asks which of the two kinds of practice a given hour contains. Chapter 3 takes up the question of hours directly.
