# Chapter 4: The Plateau Is Not the End

Sooner or later, every learner reaches a flat stretch. Progress that was steady for a long time stops, and the effort seems to buy nothing. It is easy to read this as a sign that the path has run out. The research on the subject points somewhere else. Bryan and Harter studied people learning telegraphy in 1897 and 1899.

Their measure was receiving speed. As learners practised, their speed rose, levelled off, and then rose again. The researchers read the flat stretch as a change of method inside the learner. Learners were moving from habits for single letters to habits for whole words, and the gains came back once that shift had taken hold.

The studies covered telegraph learners, not the adults this book is written for. That limit matters. A skill with its own rules may not behave the way the skill you are working on behaves. Treat the pattern as a question to test, not as a law to apply.

Still, the central idea is useful. A learning plateau can be a stage of reorganising rather than an end. When progress stops, the mind may be rebuilding how it does the task. That work is invisible from the outside, and it often feels like failure from the inside.

Most learners break through a plateau within six weeks. The book will not promise a date, and you should not expect one. Keep practising, keep notes on what changes, and give the shift time to arrive.
